# The Artist Who Reminds Us that Another Way Is Possible

One Sunday afternoon earlier this fall, I sat in the theater of Little Red School House and Elisabeth Irwin High School in Manhattan’s West Village. I was at a memorial service for the late Abigail McGrath, who was known, among other things in her extraordinary life, as “the only child” of the Harlem Renaissance. McGrath had died the previous December. Her passing was overshadowed by the winter holidays and news of the 2024 election, but she lived a life that was adventurous and beautiful and seems impossible now.

McGrath and her second husband, Anthony McGrath, were founders of the Off Center Theater, a performance group that specialized in productions our current administration would probably denounce as part of a radical leftist agenda: a drag version of Little Red Riding Hood; a play about a Puerto Rican girl who longs to be white. The McGraths’ theater was committed to bringing the arts to poor kids in New York and counts Christine Baranski and F. Murray Abraham as alums. It was a fiercely democratic vision of beauty and play that feels just out of reach at this moment.

This administration has targeted the arts by attempting to restrict National Endowment for the Arts grants, excluding writers who promote “gender ideology extremism.” It has also orchestrated the coordinated takeover of the Kennedy Center, which has traditionally operated as a pipeline for actors, dancers, directors, choreographers, and performing artists of all kinds, some of whom have gone on to make the type of discordant art that has been so crucial to the development of American culture.

Authoritarian governments view culture as a threat because in the writing and music and acting of artists, you can sometimes glimpse alternatives to the current reality. We live in a timeline that says AI domination is inevitable, there will be no future except constant war on a dead Earth, and the people with the most money will get to decide who and what survives. The arts remind us that none of the current truisms are absolutes—that there are other ways of living, existing, and being. Arts can exist outside of a tech lord’s algorithm. These disciplines operate alongside but often outside of the corporate structures that decide so much about power in the U.S. It makes sense that wresting control of the comparably meager amount of money that goes to artists is such a political obsession.

A few months before the memorial, I found myself in the hot, stuffy living room of Abigail McGrath’s house in Oak Bluffs, on Martha’s Vineyard. That week in June, the National Guard had been deployed to Los Angeles and the city’s residents were fighting to protect their neighbors from ICE agents.

Martha’s Vineyard itself was eerily quiet for that time of year. The Canadian tourists who usually arrive in early summer were largely absent—some afraid of being detained while crossing the border, some deciding to boycott the U.S. The fishermen, construction workers, cleaners, and retail workers had also begun to disappear, afraid of being taken by ICE. When I got to McGrath’s cottage, one of the repairmen there had a copy of his papers in a plastic envelope on his belt, a talisman against everything swirling around us. I needed—still need—to remember that a life of freedom and a commitment to beauty, a life like Abigail McGrath’s, is still possible.

I first came across McGrath’s name while reading a biography of Andy Warhol’s female collaborators, *Warhol’s Muses.* Mentioned only in passing, in the chapter about Warhol superstar Viva, McGrath had the distinction of being one of the few Black women who appeared in Warhol’s films. (The director Pat Hartley, the writer Dorothy Dean, and the model Donyale Luna were the others.) McGrath was in 1967’s *Tub Girls,* negotiating for the part after agreeing to source the see-through bathtub that anchored the film. That’s what drew me to her story.

I am always interested in the histories of women who have found ways to live freely—Black women especially. McGrath was the daughter of poet Helene Johnson and the niece of the celebrated Harlem Renaissance writer Dorothy West. On that June afternoon, I could see, from the porch of McGrath’s home, West’s own cottage next door. West immortalized Black Martha’s Vineyard in her 1995 novel *The Wedding,* which centers on the imminent union of a young Black woman from an educated, affluent family to a white jazz musician in mid-century America—a brief outline of the McGraths’ own relationship.

McGrath’s cottage is in the Highlands, known as the Pork Chop to some locals. It’s called the Pork Chop to distinguish it from the tonier, whiter neighborhoods of West Chop and East Chop. In *The Wedding,* West refers to the Pork Chop as “the Oval.” This particular version of Martha’s Vineyard may have paved the way for its current iteration as the vacation spot for practitioners of Black excellence. But the Vineyard of this narrow, winding road in the Pork Chop is older than that of the Obamas and Skip Gates. The bramble bushes are overgrown; the food comes from the Cape Verdean, Aquinnah Wampanoag, Jamaican, and Black American communities that have worked and lived on the island for a more than a hundred years.

McGrath’s cottage is modest from the outside, and the rooms are small. When I visited, her family was in the midst of renovations; the air smelled of fresh sawdust. The living room was stacked with boxes, some containing parts to build writing desks. In her later years, McGrath ran a residency here, Renaissance House, dedicated to supporting working-class writers. It is part of a Vineyard that is decidedly less polished and more nonconformist than the fantasies of infinity pools and white parties—more wild and beautiful and spiky and definitely more queer.

“Dorothy was as queer as a grapefruit,” McGrath’s eldest son, Jason Rosen, tells me. “Everyone in our family was queer.”

“The first time I met him,” Rosen’s wife, Agnes, says of her husband, “he was in drag, playing Little Red Riding Hood in one of his parents’ productions.”

Jason and Agnes gamely agreed to meet with me, a complete stranger, when I contacted them about visiting the house.

McGrath was called the only child of the Harlem Renaissance because so many of the artists of that movement were queer: Langston Hughes, Gladys Bentley, Ma Rainey, Alain LeRoy Locke, and Dorothy West herself. “Of all the writers during [that] period, my mother was the only one to have a child,” McGrath later told a writer for warholstars.org, a site documenting the people who starred in Warhol’s films.

As McGrath described it, “Here we have a poet mom raising a child on her own—not in NYC, as was her dream, but in Brooklyn, as was her reality. We are talking educated poor here. Think about it: How many wealthy poets do you know? It is easier to get a job as a meteorologist than as a poet. Living with a poet is a lot of fun because you never know that you are poor. I went to a prestigious private school”—Little Red School House, where her memorial was held—“[and] we went to Broadway theater, the opera. We took advantage of everything the city had to offer on a salary that was lunch money for the parents of my schoolmates.”

The educated poor is a class that used to make up the creative life of New York City, of so many pockets of innovation across this country. A reasonable cost of living and local and national programs designed to making a dignified life accessible for more Americans gave people the ability to provide for themselves and their families while still living creatively.

In *The Wedding,* West writes, “Identity is not inherent. It is shaped by circumstance and sensitivity and resistance to self-pity.” Black American creativity, that brand of Black excellence that is so popular to celebrate, is not inherent to our culture. It can be difficult to trace the ways institutions shape a life. The lives of the Black artists who make up Black excellence were shaped by the institutions of the past century, which were rooted in a belief in a common public good and a deep appreciation for the history of artistic craft. We are living through the destruction of all that. I wonder if this century could produce an Abigail McGrath. I am not so sure.

After high school, McGrath went to Bard College, where she met and married fellow student Leonard Rosen. “I entered Bard a virgin and came out a mother with a two-year-old child,” she told the Warhol blog. When she left Bard, she took that child, Jason, and moved to Paris to work briefly at the Folies Bergère as a mannequin, since she had “two left feet” and could not dance or sing. Unable to fully support herself and her son, she moved back to New York, working at the coat check and as a bouncer at Max’s Kansas City, where, she once claimed, she hummed a tune while Lou Reed walked by that ended up in “Walk on the Wild Side.” Wanting to be an actor and director, she made *Tub Girls* with Warhol.

McGrath joined a theater troupe downtown and met Anthony McGrath, a fellow actor 12 years her senior who would become her husband and creative partner. The two formed a relationship, and they later had a son, Benson. The troupe served as a staging ground for generations of young artists. During the summers, the family went back to Martha’s Vineyard and lived in the house beside West’s.

West had retreated to Martha’s Vineyard as the Harlem Renaissance ended in the 1930s. She was 18 years old when she won second place in a writing contest, went to Harlem for the awards dinner, and met Langston Hughes and Zora Neale Hurston. Hughes called her “the Kid,” and she crashed on Hurston’s couch—though Hurston complained to friends that West would rarely pay her bills.

McGrath’s mother, Helene Johnson, followed her to Manhattan in 1927, publishing her poetry in magazines including *Vanity Fair.* West and Johnson had been raised as sisters. They did not even know they were cousins until they entered high school. They were the black sheep of the family, Jason Rosen tells me. West and Johnson lived in New York, not Boston. They were unmarried artists. West would write about the colorism and classism of her Black Boston Brahmin family in her 1948 novel *The Living Is Easy.*It was a history she would turn to again and again in her work.

The West family started vacationing in Martha’s Vineyard back when Oak Bluffs was home to a long-running tent revival camp. On the Vineyard, West lived with her longtime girlfriend Marian Minus, a fellow writer. Together, West and Minus ran *Challenge,* a leftist literary journal that championed Black women writers in the 1930s. Minus was a Marxist, a political identity that some speculate led to her later exclusion from accountings of the Renaissance. West herself downplayed her political affiliations. “You can’t make a good communist out of me,” she told an interviewer in 1988. “Then again, you can’t make a good anything out of me.”

West was an iconoclast to the end. Her cottage is even smaller than McGrath’s, with a narrow double-front door and a porch where West worked on *The Wedding.* When West was experiencing a new wave of literary stardom in the 1990s, this was where she would entertain visitors like Oprah Winfrey and Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis, the latter of whom edited *The Wedding* and agreed to West’s stipulation that they work only from her home in the Pork Chop.

Across the street from McGrath’s house, there is a field about a quarter of the size of a city block. It is overrun with grass and wildflowers.

Jason and Agnes tell me that their family wants to turn the lot into a public park and host readings and performances there. “The town is excited,” Jason says. “They don’t get many opportunities to have a new park.” It’s late afternoon when I leave, and I can hear the crickets starting up. It’s the eternal soundtrack to a summer evening, probably the same one that McGrath and West heard here when they were alive. Maybe the writers and artists and readers who congregate in this field in some evening in the future will hear it too.
