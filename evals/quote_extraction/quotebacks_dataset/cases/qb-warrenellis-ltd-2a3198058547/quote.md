> Within [ghost hunting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_hunting "Ghost hunting") and [parapsychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parapsychology "Parapsychology"), **electronic voice phenomena** (**EVP**) are sounds found on electronic recordings that are interpreted as spirit voices. Parapsychologist [Konstantīns Raudive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstant%C4%ABns_Raudive "Konstantīns Raudive"), who popularized the idea in the 1970s, described EVP as typically brief, usually the length of a word or short phrase.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon#cite_note-bretf-1)
>
> Enthusiasts consider EVP to be a form of [paranormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranormal "Paranormal") phenomenon often found in recordings with [static](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_noise "White noise") or other [background noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Background_noise "Background noise"). Scientists regard EVP as a form of auditory [pareidolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareidolia "Pareidolia") (interpreting random sounds as voices in one’s own language) and a [pseudoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscience "Pseudoscience") promulgated by popular culture.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon#cite_note-Williams2013-2)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon#cite_note-Anderson-3) Prosaic explanations for EVP include [apophenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apophenia "Apophenia") (perceiving patterns in random information), equipment artifacts, and hoaxes
