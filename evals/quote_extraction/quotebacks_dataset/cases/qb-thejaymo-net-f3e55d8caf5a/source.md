# Using personal weeknotes as a tool for attention

Since leaving my day job two years ago, I’ve been writing [personal weeknotes](https://tracydurnell.com/category/weeknotes/). In short: once a week, I publish on my blog a set of notes about what I did the past week. I believe weeknotes started inside organizations, but **I appreciate using the weekly checkpoint as a personal tool to steer my attention and action**.

*This post is my entry for July’s [IndieWeb Carnival](https://indieweb.org/indieweb-carnival) [on the theme of tools](https://jamesg.blog/2024/07/01/indieweb-carnival-tools/), hosted by James G. [Crossposted to IndieNews](https://news.indieweb.org/en).*

## Why I started keeping personal weeknotes

I knew, as I was transitioning from an externally-structured schedule to a [self-defined one](https://tracydurnell.com/2023/03/18/swimming-outside-the-lanes/), that I would be [susceptible to feelings](https://tracydurnell.com/2022/02/22/the-reckoning-of-the-to-do-list/) of [not doing enough](https://tracydurnell.com/2023/02/20/the-work-is-not-enough/). It’s easy for me to trip over issues of self-worth related to work and worries about carrying my fair share in my partnership. (Fortunately, my partner has been my biggest cheerleader for both betting on myself and acknowledging that we don’t need to “pencil out” our contributions to be equal partners.) As I started my journey of self-employment, I needed to [balance](https://tracydurnell.com/2022/09/14/rest-ethic/) those fears of [not doing enough](https://tracydurnell.com/2023/10/08/accepting-the-capacity-gap/) with [my state of burnout and exhaustion](https://tracydurnell.com/2023/05/24/assuming-no-change-is-an-option/), taking time to rest while also making progress.

I noticed other bloggers writing weeknotes, both professional and personal. At first, I was simply interested in the contents of what people were doing — and then I recognized the usefulness of weeknotes as a tool.

### Weeknotes are a multipurpose tool

Weeknotes are a **tool for awareness**, helping me learn what I’m capable of doing in a week so I can make reasonable plans for the future — both for work and life. They also serve as a record of what I \*did\* do, reminding me of things that I might have forgotten.

Weeknotes let me **externalize my internal goals**. I’ve had trouble sticking to goals I’ve set for myself in the past, with external accountability being a much more powerful driver. They provide **gentle accountability** through publishing them publicly. Committing to publishing gives me a specific time to do them and helps me follow through on actually completing the weeknotes. Some of the things I track are both useful to me *and*  a way to participate in [indie web discovery](https://tracydurnell.com/2024/05/24/indie-web-curation-and-collation/), which gives me another nudge.

Weeknotes are a means to **shape my own behavior**. **Headings serve as prompts**: knowing I have a slot in the template waiting for me to fill in on Fridays is a reminder — and motivation — for me to do things that I value but might not make time for (like listen to new music). **Because I design my own format, weeknotes reinforce *my* priorities.**

### What I like about personal weeknotes as a tool

I’m good about starting the week off with a to-do list, but I had trouble getting any end-of-week process to stick. Weeknotes are what’s finally worked for me. It’s become a **marking point for me to divide time** since I work from home, so weeks and months can easily blur together since I lack physical cues.

I’ve experimented with a variety of accountability methods, including monthly and quarterly check-ins, but **the weekly cadence influences my behavior whereas longer time periods become retrospective documentation**. For me, the once-a-week rhythm is what makes weeknotes a *tool* versus merely a record.

I have a tendency to get locked into formats prematurely, but treating weeknotes as a tool gives me the mental wiggle room to **change the format as suits me**.

## Creating my personal weeknote template

### First version: life categories

[For the first three months](https://cascadiainspired.com/week-notes-sabbatical-week-1/), I made notes organized by a set of life categories: creative work, career, play, learning, household, and health.

I’d chosen these “buckets” through my [personal annual review process](https://cascadiainspired.com/category/annual-review/), and used them for quarterly check-ins to make sure I stayed on track through the year. For weeknotes, though, I found them **too vague**, which meant that I tracked either too much or too little. At a quarterly scale, it was easier to identify what was “worth” keeping track of, but on a weekly basis, there were things I did that didn’t fit nicely into one category or another, and things I wanted to “give myself credit for” that didn’t seem big enough. The “demands” of each life category cycle at a different pace, too, not necessarily aligned with weeks.

By the time my post-quitting “sabbatical” ended, I recognized that format wasn’t what I needed.

### Version two: specific actions

I [restarted weeknotes](https://tracydurnell.com/2022/12/09/weeknotes-dec-3-9/) here in a different format. I can’t recall who I borrowed inspiration for the format from, but I started with:

- stuff I did
- what I read
- changes to my website

Since then, I’ve added more headings:

- words I looked up and concepts I learned
- pretty things I saw
- what I ate for dinner
- new music I listened to
- nature notes
- win of the week
- looking forward to

**Being specific makes the tool easy to use** — I don’t have to debate whether something belongs.

Some headings — new music, website changes, nature notes, words — might not have something every week, but I keep them in the template as a prompt. I tried out a few other headings that didn’t stick, and have a few floating headings that aren’t in the template but I’ll add on if it comes up.

## Why I log what I log

### Setting reasonable expectations

I wanted to track “stuff I did” to help myself **recognize how much happens in a week and set healthier self expectations**. My two actual “metrics” are how many hours I spent on paid consulting work (or sometimes business development) and how many hours I spent writing fiction. Everything else just gets a bullet point.

**I don’t track anything that could make me feel *actually* bad about myself.** I might be a little embarrassed if I don’t write as much as I wanted to, but no one’s going to judge me as a person for it — creative folks get it. The intention of my weeknotes [is not to shame myself](https://tracydurnell.com/2024/07/14/self-care-thats-not-driven-by-shame/).

### All my reading, not just what I finished

[I care about how much I read](https://tracydurnell.com/2023/08/19/my-reading-philosophy-and-17-reading-guidelines/), both because it signifies that I’ve made time for leisure and because I’m a fiction writer so reading stories supports storycraft. While I still log reads on Goodreads, I consider my website the canonical record of my reading. In my weeknotes, I track what I finished reading as well as what I spent time reading, what I quit reading, and any books I bought. Having each week’s reading in a condensed bullet point format means I notice right away if I haven’t been reading enough.

Originally, I also listed what I added to my to-read list, but that became annoying to cross-reference from Goodreads — and it made me feel a little guilty about [adding so many things to my TBR](https://tracydurnell.com/2023/11/02/appreciating-books-i-havent-read-yet/), which isn’t what I intended. **The nice thing about tools you make for yourself is that you get to change them when they don’t work the way you thought.**

### Pinpointing definitions

After reading *Several Short Sentences About Writing* and [*On Tyranny*](https://tracydurnell.com/2022/06/09/read-on-tyranny-graphic-edition/), I wanted to pay closer attention to [the specific meanings of words](https://tracydurnell.com/2023/03/25/the-tactic-of-destroying-the-meaning-of-words/), especially [which words and phrases I use in my own writing](https://tracydurnell.com/2022/12/28/meaning-through-truthfulness-in-language/).

Having a place to log the words and concepts I learn makes me **engage more closely with what I’m reading**. I notice more often when I’ve gleaned the meaning of a word from context, but don’t *really* know what it means. Sometimes I’ll try to define a word out loud to my husband (sorry babe), which typically makes undeniable the fact that I don’t know what I’m talking about 😂

**The way I compile my weeknotes reinforces the goal** to learn definitions more precisely. I add words to a draft post throughout the week, but I intentionally don’t link the definitions when I add them. That means I need to look up each word again at the end of the week to add the link. Before I look it up, I try to remember the definition — usually I’m wrong, so being forced to look at it a second time helps me learn it better.

(As I’ve been tracking [new-to-me words](https://tracydurnell.com/resources/words/), I have come to notice just how many are [bullshit](https://tracydurnell.com/2024/02/13/the-many-tongues-of-english/). Writers adjectivize or adverbify complex terms that I can barely understand on their own, let alone adapted into a modifier. (I *beg* you not to use the term synechdochical. I have looked it up So Many Times and I still don’t get it 🤦‍♀️😂) I am on board with the concept of words having a cost — and that a writer only has budget for a few $5 words per post or essay. Wanting to sound erudite (there goes my budget) leads us to [over-complicate the language we use](https://tracydurnell.com/2023/02/13/everyone-prefers-plain-language/). We should probably skip the $10 word, and the $25 word is just showing off.)

### A record of dinners

Choosing what to eat for dinner is the most contentious issue in my household because neither of us wants to decide 😂 I hoped that tracking what we ate would provide inspiration for future meals — it hasn’t happened thus far but that’s not to say it won’t 😉 The log does serve as a **handy reference**, though, since I link to any new recipes I make. I also look back when I can’t remember how old leftovers are 😄

### Participating in the indie web

While Instagram and Pinterest are guaranteed sources for visual inspiration, [I hate the world of infinite scroll](https://tracydurnell.com/2023/07/31/read-stolen-focus/). Giving myself a place to curate pretty things I’ve seen on the broader indie web **supports the indie mode of discovery** for anyone reading, and also **makes it easier for me** to celebrate cool things without making a dedicated post about it. It **encourages me to delve\* deeper** into an artist’s portfolio and explore their other work on their website.

\*(I hear ‘delve’ is a genAI indicator now but it’s too good a word to give up 😜 You can’t have it, learning machines!)

### Discovering new music without a streaming subscription

Cancelling my music streaming service [disrupted the system I used to discover new music](https://tracydurnell.com/2023/04/25/listening-to-music-without-a-streaming-subscription/) over the previous decade. I [value listening to new music](https://tracydurnell.com/2022/08/19/new-music/), so this heading reminds me to make the effort.

It takes [more intentionality](https://tracydurnell.com/2024/05/09/taste-requires-intentional-attention/) to re-listen to songs without a streaming service, since there isn’t one place where all the new music I’ve tried lives; having a record lets me go back if I want to listen to a specific song again instead of being lost to the ether.

Even though I post all the new music I listen to the entire track, so it’s not precisely an endorsement, I still think it [**contributes to indie web discovery**](https://tracydurnell.com/2021/11/11/breaking-out-of-what-the-algorithm-feeds-you/). Having access to — and choosing from — the world’s entire pool of music is overwhelming, which is why discovery on streaming services happens [via algorithm](https://tracydurnell.com/2023/11/07/in-algorithm-we-trust/). A smaller set of options to sample from is way more human-friendly. I appreciate when other bloggers suggest music to try, so this is another **pay-it-forward category**.

### Watching my wildlife garden

I turned my yard into a wildlife garden, and now I see animals all the time 😃🥰 Having this section reminds me to pay attention to what’s happening outside in my yard — I’ve identified more birds in the past couple months than previous years 😲 ([Moving the birdbath](https://cascadiainspired.com/imperfection-through-gardening/) in front of the kitchen window helped too.) These are birds that \*live in my backyard\* so I could have been spotting them all along 😅 It’s **a prompt to immerse myself in my physical space**: to notice what’s changed, and to appreciate the results of all my and my husband’s hard work.

I also think the science of ecological timing — [phenology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenology) — is extremely cool and [important in a world of climate change](https://tracydurnell.com/2022/10/17/climate-change-makes-animal-populations-more-susceptible-to-other-impacts/). It’s also [an easy thing for hobbyists to track](https://tracydurnell.com/2024/02/19/things-to-do-with-a-website/). I only added this heading this spring, so I’m looking forward to building up more than a year’s log so I can compare timing between years!

### Choosing highlights

I pick a win for the week and identify something I’m looking forward to. This is **a practice in positivity** that forces me to reflect on the week, specifically looking for the good stuff rather than dwelling on the bad. It’s also a **reminder** to plan things to look forward to. These prompts are both challenging for me, so they’re usually the last thing I complete in my weeknotes each week 😉
