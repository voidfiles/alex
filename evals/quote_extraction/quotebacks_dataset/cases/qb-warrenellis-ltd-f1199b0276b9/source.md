# Reissue of the Week: Do The Impossible: Original Soundtrack, by Sun Ra | The Quietus

&amp;lt;a href="https://sunra-mh.bandcamp.com/album/do-the-impossible-original-soundtrack-to-the-documentary"&amp;gt;Do The Impossible: Original Soundtrack To The Documentary by Sun Ra&amp;lt;/a&amp;gt;

Among his many mythopoeic pronouncements, the great Sun Ra maintained that he was making music for the future. So, we shouldn’t be surprised that, a quarter of the way through the 21st century, and more than 30 years after he left the planet for the last time, his music is gaining more recognition than ever before. A recent slew of reissues, new compilations and previously unheard live recordings continues unabated. His Arkestra – under the direction of long-time member, alto saxophonist Marshall Allen since 1995 – continues to delight audiences worldwide. Just last year saw the release of two new feature-length documentaries about him. *The Magic City* examined his early life in Birmingham, Alabama; and the more comprehensive *Sun Ra: Do The Impossible* did the festival circuit before being shown on the PBS TV network this February as part of its *American Masters* series of artist biographies – bringing Ra’s improbable story to its widest audience yet.

The documentary, *Do The Impossible,* follows the trajectory of Ra’s artistic life: his formative years as Herman Poole Blount in Birmingham; his early transfiguration and sudden gift of the knowledge that he, in fact, came from Saturn; his mid-50s move to Chicago, where he took the name Sun Ra and formed the Arkestra, recruiting stalwarts including Allen, tenor saxophonist John Gilmore and baritone saxophonist Pat Patrick; his relocation to New York in the early 60s, where he helped spearhead the nascent free jazz New Thing; and, in the 70s, the Arkestra’s settling in Philadelphia and beginning to reach large European jazz festival audiences. The movie is also a deft study in his multi-faceted personality and otherworldly philosophies, best summarised by poet and scholar Fred Moten:

*“I believe that it is genuinely a mischaracterisation to speak of one Sun Ra. At any given time, there was a whole bunch more. Sun Ra the virtuoso, teacher, bandleader, the poet, cosmologist, historian, the Egyptologist, community leader, the patriarch, the mother and that’s just scratching the surface.”*

The movie features a trove of audio and video clips of Ra expounding his living myth, soundbites from talking heads including Moten, other academics and musicians, and copious snippets of music, both studio recordings and live performances – all of which can be heard on this new soundtrack album. But how to accurately represent Ra’s wildly diverse output over 40 years from the mid-50s to his final ascension in 1993? Across that oeuvre, we find an aesthetic containing just as many multitudes as Moten’s character thumbnail. His work – and a typical Arkestra show today – touches on Big Band stomps, deep modal excursions, driving hard bop, wafting space ballads, philosophical-poetic declamations, group chants, free jazz and even weirder experimentation. How to fit all this into an album lasting an hour and a quarter?

The solution is a largely unsatisfactory one. All of the music (and some spoken word) from the film is presented in the same order as heard in the movie but sidesteps any concrete sense of chronology. Almost all of the tracks are extremely short – most between two and three minutes, some even less. While that does make room for some punchy all-time bangers including ‘Angels And Demons At Play’ and ‘Interplanetary Music’, other major statements are brutally abridged. ‘Spontaneous Simplicity’ – a modal fantasy usually running to eight minutes – is slashed to two minutes. ‘Door To The Cosmos’ – a nine-minute slab of languid funk – is cut to just a minute and a half. In both cases, it completely negates their dreamy insistence and sense of infinite exploration.

One point the documentary makes early on is Ra’s love of and debt to the big band sounds of the 30s and 40s. He talks about witnessing Duke Ellington play in Birmingham in his youth, and of his infatuation with the music of Fletcher Henderson. He saw these bands as paragons of Black identity, community and self-worth at a time when so much was denied to so many in a racially segregated US. “Above all,” he says, “I liked the discipline of Fletcher Henderson and no one else has ever come up to that. They were so together, I begin to say they couldn’t possibly be men. They must have been angels.” It’s a strand that’s well-represented on the compilation, with a rousing Arkestral romp through Henderson’s ‘Big John’s Special’, and classic Ra tunes ‘El Is A Sound Of Joy’ and ‘Space Is The Place’ given a brass-heavy big band treatment – crowd-pleasing live performances largely divested of the essential mystery of the original studio cuts.

The compilation also fixates on an area of Ra’s work perhaps best described as exotica. It was a key component of his 50s sound, before his space age fascination really took hold, at a time when he was obsessed with making connections to ancestral African civilisations. There’s the clopping doo-wop of 1955’s ‘Dreaming’, recorded with vocal group The Cosmic Rays (though his involvement with doo-wop and R&B is left otherwise unexplored). But it’s best exemplified by 1959’s ‘Ancient Aethiopia’ – the longest cut on the album at just over six minutes. It’s a majestic anthem of regal horns, pounding tympani, cymbal splashes and darting flutes suggesting a royal barge slowly navigating a wide river, which flautist Nicole Mitchell rightly identifies as part of a search for diasporic mysteries and pre-slavery spiritual traditions.

*Do The Impossible* finds room for more experimental sounds too. There are brief snatches of Ra’s wildly unpredictable solo piano recitals. His early championing of electronic keyboards is touched on with 1956’s jittery ‘Advice for Medics. And a couple of very short excerpts – less than two minutes each – give fleeting glimpses of Ra’s volcanic engagement with the MOOG synthesizer, transporting exercises in pure sound art acting more as portals to alter-dimensions than mere music.

But one glaring omission from the collection is free jazz. It’s clear that this compilation is not aimed at die-hard Ra heads but has been assembled for a newly switched-on audience who might find the ecstatic peaks of a 20-piece Arkestra blowing wild and free perhaps just a little too challenging. Yet it’s a major part of his story. He was undoubtedly a key pioneer of the free jazz movement, having played with a sextet at the October Revolution in Jazz, the four-day concert series organised by trumpeter Bill Dixon in New York in 1964, which also included performances by Milford Graves, John Tchicai, Burton Greene and others, and which effectively announced the arrival of the New Thing.

With typical ambiguity, Ra never fully subscribed to the idea of total formlessness in music, scornfully referring to other free jazz musicians as the “freedom boys” and claiming that his own experiments in seeming spontaneity were underpinned by strict discipline (neatly linking back to his admiration for Fletcher Henderson). For him, so-called free jazz was a vehicle to liberation from constraints – physical, emotional, philosophical, artistic – but, like any spiritual practice, only achievable through hard work. It was a bold challenge to humanity and one which, in many ways, we’re still catching up on. Through his music, Ra is still operating in this realm, outside of time, trying to get us there.

“My story is endless,” he pronounces in the album’s final moments. “It never repeats itself. I’m not part of history. I’m more a part of a mystery.”
