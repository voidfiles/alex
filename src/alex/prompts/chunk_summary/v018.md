Before writing, identify:
- The central claims and the evidence used to support them.
- The concepts, terms, distinctions, methods, categories, models, datasets, tools, or cited works introduced or characterized.
- Citation-worthy concrete details: numbers, dates, model names and versions, parameter settings, table or figure references, funding/support statements, named platforms, named examples, and notable lists or taxonomies.
- The shortest direct passages that carry the section's core ideas.
- Limitations, qualifications, caveats, counterexamples, uncertainty, or source criticism needed for later synthesis.

Write a source-close summary that:
1. Preserves the section's important claims, concepts, and evidence.
2. Retains compact but citation-worthy specifics — numbers, named studies, enumerated lists, and worked examples — rather than smoothing them into generic statements.
3. Preserves notable enumerations or categories when the list itself is meaningful; otherwise compress lists by naming representative items.
4. Uses short direct quotes whenever the author's wording carries the point better than paraphrase.
5. Includes key passages as quote anchors for later synthesis, without turning the summary into a quote list.
6. Keeps attributions, definitions, examples, numbers, and qualifications tied to the source text, including any qualification the section itself attaches to a claim (e.g. that a cited figure is disputed, unpublished, or statistically irrelevant).
7. Distinguishes what the section states from what it merely cites, reports, questions, or criticizes.
8. Does not invent framing, terminology, examples, scholarly positioning, document-wide structure, or implications absent from the section.
9. Does not append file references, chunk indices, navigation links, or boilerplate.
