## Dimension definitions

### Coherence

Coherence concerns how clearly ideas connect at the sentence, paragraph, and whole-document levels.

Consider:
- Whether the summary maintains an identifiable through-line
- Whether each paragraph develops a clear idea
- Whether claims and explanations connect logically
- Whether transitions make relationships between ideas apparent
- Whether pronouns, references, and conceptual terms have clear antecedents
- Whether shifts in topic, scope, or abstraction are adequately signaled
- Whether the conclusion follows naturally from the preceding discussion

Do not score coherence based on whether the ideas are factually correct. Judge whether they form an intelligible and internally connected account.

Scoring anchors:

- 5 — Excellent: The through-line is immediately clear and sustained throughout. Sentences and paragraphs build naturally on one another, transitions are purposeful, and virtually no logical or referential gaps interrupt comprehension.
- 4 — Strong: The argument is easy to follow, with only occasional abrupt transitions, weak connections, or minor referential ambiguity.
- 3 — Adequate: The main direction is understandable, but several connections are implicit, transitions are mechanical or uneven, or some paragraphs feel only loosely connected.
- 2 — Weak: The through-line is difficult to sustain. Ideas frequently appear disconnected, relationships are unclear, or abrupt shifts substantially impede comprehension.
- 1 — Poor: The summary lacks an intelligible through-line. Ideas are fragmented, contradictory in presentation, or arranged without discernible logical connection.

### Organization

Organization concerns the macro-level arrangement and proportion of the document.

Consider:
- Whether the opening establishes an appropriate frame
- Whether sections and paragraphs appear in a useful sequence
- Whether related material is grouped together
- Whether headings accurately describe and distinguish their contents
- Whether the document avoids unnecessary fragmentation
- Whether important ideas receive proportionate space
- Whether repetition results from poor placement or duplicated sections
- Whether the conclusion provides closure rather than merely stopping or repeating the introduction

Distinguish organization from coherence:
- Coherence asks whether ideas connect.
- Organization asks whether those ideas are placed and grouped effectively.

Do not penalize the summary for omitting source content. Judge only the arrangement of the material it includes.

Scoring anchors:

- 5 — Excellent: The structure strongly supports comprehension. Sections are purposeful, sequencing is intuitive, related ideas are consolidated, emphasis is proportionate, and the ending provides effective closure.
- 4 — Strong: The structure works well overall, with only minor issues such as a slightly misplaced paragraph, unnecessary heading, uneven section length, or modest repetition.
- 3 — Adequate: The structure is usable but conventional, uneven, or somewhat mechanical. Some material could be regrouped, reordered, condensed, or given clearer hierarchy.
- 2 — Weak: Structural problems are recurring. Sections overlap, ordering obscures relationships, headings fragment the discussion, or important material is buried or disproportionately treated.
- 1 — Poor: The document has little functional structure. Ordering appears arbitrary, sections do not serve clear purposes, and substantial reorganization is necessary.

### Readability

Readability concerns the clarity, precision, economy, and fluency of the prose.

Consider:
- Sentence clarity and manageable syntactic complexity
- Precision and consistency of wording
- Appropriate use of technical language
- Avoidance of vague abstractions and inflated phrasing
- Avoidance of filler, padding, throat-clearing, and generic academic language
- Avoidance of redundant statements
- Paragraph length and information density
- Grammatical fluency
- Ease of reading for an academically literate audience

Complex ideas and specialized terminology are not inherently unreadable. Penalize complexity only when the prose makes the ideas harder to understand than necessary.

Distinguish readability from coherence:
- Readability asks whether individual passages are clearly and efficiently expressed.
- Coherence asks whether the ideas form a connected whole.

Scoring anchors:

- 5 — Excellent: The prose is consistently clear, precise, fluent, and economical. Complexity is handled without unnecessary density, filler, repetition, or awkwardness.
- 4 — Strong: The prose is clear and professional, with occasional verbosity, dense sentences, vague wording, or minor repetition that does not meaningfully hinder reading.
- 3 — Adequate: The prose is understandable but regularly wordy, abstract, repetitive, formulaic, or syntactically cumbersome. Editing would noticeably improve efficiency and clarity.
- 2 — Weak: Dense, vague, repetitive, or awkward prose frequently slows comprehension. Many sentences or paragraphs require rereading.
- 1 — Poor: The prose is consistently confusing, severely awkward, excessively repetitive, or otherwise difficult to understand.

## Scoring rules

- Assign an integer from 1 to 5 for each dimension.
- Score each dimension independently; do not force the scores to match.
- Use 3 for genuinely adequate work, not as a default when uncertain.
- Reserve 5 for writing with no material weakness in that dimension.
- A few isolated imperfections are compatible with a 4.
- Repeated or document-wide problems generally warrant a 3 or below.
- Do not reward length, formality, numerous headings, or sophisticated vocabulary by themselves.
- Do not infer writing defects merely because the subject matter is complex.
- Base the score on the dominant reading experience, while noting any major local exception.

In the notes:
- Identify the most consequential writing strengths or weaknesses.
- Explain why the scores differ, when they differ.
- Refer concretely to patterns in the summary rather than giving generic feedback.
- Do not discuss factual correctness, source coverage, scholarship, citations, or links.
- Keep the notes to one concise paragraph.
