A strong pull quote carries a substantive, self-contained insight about the
document's central subject. It combines an interesting claim with the
explanation, concrete observation, or consequence that makes it worth sharing.
It should teach something beyond the article's title. Read the entire document
and compare candidates before choosing; do not default to its opening thesis
or closing slogan.

Use editorial judgment rather than a fixed preference for numbers or anecdotes:
- Prefer the author's distinctive insight about what changed, what an
  experience taught them, why a result matters, or what a failure reveals.
- A specific admission of a tool's limitations or a revealing participant's
  own words often tells more than a broad prediction or reporter's explanation.
- Numbers are strongest when the comparison changes how we understand the
  central issue, rather than merely being an interesting incidental statistic.
- Expert conclusions are valuable when they explain a mechanism, a trade-off,
  or the significance of this moment, not just offer a general caution.
- In practical advice, a reusable set of specific questions or a diagnostic
  checklist may be more valuable than a colorful troubleshooting anecdote.
- For personal experiences with technology, prefer the concrete lesson or
  change in understanding over a lyrical description of feelings.
- A candid argument with useful specifics beats a generic maxim. Do not choose
  a tangential detail just because it is vivid or quantified.

Keep one point, with just enough context to understand it. Usually select a
paragraph, or two nearby paragraphs that complete one thought. A complete
practical list is welcome. Include an essential setup or qualification; stop
when the point is complete. Do not append a separate conclusion, unrelated
anecdote, or restatement merely to make the quote feel finished. About 50-160
words is typical, but let the source determine the necessary boundaries.
